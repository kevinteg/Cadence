**The prune reflex proved more durable than the build reflex.** Across three of the four shipped pursuits, the opening move was to add something — more verbs, more architecture, more entities — and the delivered move was to remove it. `build-cadence-v1` proposed Sessions, Markers, per-agent voice registers, and DoD checklists; it shipped with the Session concept gone, a single voice register set by the invoked verb, and Intent-and-Actions replacing the checklist. `improve-ux-and-vision` opened with a list of gaps to fill and closed by deleting the Idea entity entirely, collapsing three verbs into one, and stripping a vision document of claims that were never built. Even `make-cadence-public` spent meaningful time removing a standalone MCP client it had just written. The lesson is not that building is wrong — it's that accumulation is the default failure mode, and the pursuits that shipped cleanly were the ones that recognized "prune and sharpen" as a legitimate first answer rather than a retreat.

**The data model rewrites were the highest-leverage moves.** The voice collapse in v1, the Intent-and-Actions migration, the retirement of the Idea entity, the Inbox redefinition as a view rather than a directory — each of these looked structural and expensive when proposed, and each of them simplified every downstream surface they touched. The Idea entity retirement, in particular, is instructive: deleting `IdeaSchema`, three verbs, and dozens of files enabled the brainstorm-as-workspace redesign, which gave users a cleaner onboarding story than the entity model ever had. The pattern is that data model decisions compound in both directions. Correct models reduce the surface every verb has to navigate; incorrect ones accumulate workarounds until the workarounds become the product.

**Validation failures shaped better abstractions than design sessions.** The MCP integration's first design — a standalone TypeScript client with its own transport and configuration registry — was coherent on paper and wrong on contact. OAuth tokens lived in Claude Code's Keychain layer, not in a standalone process's reach. The user's critique named the division of responsibility correctly: the Cadence CLI handles local file-hierarchy mutations; MCP is network I/O with credentials. That failure produced a cleaner architectural rule ("agent initiative" vs. "user direction") that now applies to all ambient tooling, not just MCP. The same pattern appeared during `build-cadence-v1` when the promote-state-verbs-to-commands project was created and dropped after walking each candidate verb individually — real assessment produced a "low-leverage" judgment that design-time analysis had missed. The pursuing-and-then-abandoning pattern is not waste; it's the cost of loading real constraints.

**Execution-only pursuits moved faster than discovery-oriented ones.** `make-cadence-public` opened with concrete scope, ran four projects in parallel, and archived with no brainstorms opened under it. `build-cadence-v1` ran 47 projects over six weeks with significant structural pivots mid-arc. The signal is not that brainstorming is slow — the brainstorm-workspace rebuild in `improve-ux-and-vision` was high-leverage — but that divergence phases incur cost, and when the problem is already clear, the best move is to start working it. The closing-in-on-resolution prompt added during `improve-ux-and-vision` was a direct response to this: finalization work (audit, narrative, capstone) was landing as a surprise at the end of pursuits rather than as a planned phase. Building it into upward completion was a structural acknowledgment that the cost pattern was predictable and had been ignored.
